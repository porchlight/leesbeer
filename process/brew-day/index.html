Brew Day

This is the big day. Plan for about 4-5 hours from start to cleanup. Have your recipe pulled up, your grains crushed, and your hops weighed out before you start. A typical 5 gallon all-grain batch on the Brewzilla looks like this:

The night before

Do yourself a favor and prep the night before:

  • Crush your grains (or confirm they're pre-crushed) and put them in a bucket
  • Weigh out your hops into separate bags/bowls labeled with when they go in (60 min, 15 min, flameout, etc.)
  • Weigh out your brewing salts
  • Make sure your yeast is ready (if using dry yeast, just have the packet out; some people like to let it come to room temperature)
  • Read through your recipe one more time

Step 1: Water and heating

Fill your Brewzilla with your strike water. For a typical 5 gallon batch, you'll want around 8-8.5 gallons (30-32 liters) of total water. The Brewzilla has volume markings on the inside to help.

The exact amount depends on your recipe and your system's boil-off rate. The Brewzilla 35L typically boils off about 1-1.5 gallons per hour. You'll figure out your exact numbers after a couple brews.

Water chemistry: If you're adjusting your water profile, now is the time to add your brewing salts (gypsum, calcium chloride, etc.) and any acid (lactic acid or hydrochloric acid) to hit your target mash pH of around 5.2-5.4. Stir them in well.

Set the Brewzilla to your strike temperature. This is usually about 3-5°F higher than your target mash temperature because the grain will cool the water down when you add it. So if your recipe calls for a 152°F mash, set the Brewzilla to about 155-157°F.

Turn on the pump to help circulate and heat the water evenly. The Brewzilla will take about 30-45 minutes to heat up depending on your starting water temp.

Step 2: Mashing

Once your strike water hits temperature:

  1. Insert the malt pipe into the Brewzilla. Make sure the bottom screen/plate is in place.
  2. Turn off the pump while you add grains.
  3. Slowly pour in your grain bill, stirring constantly with your brew paddle. You want to break up any doughballs -- clumps of dry grain that won't convert properly. Take your time here, don't just dump it all in at once.
  4. Place the top screen on top of the grain bed.
  5. Set the Brewzilla to your mash temperature (typically 148-156°F depending on your recipe -- lower for a drier beer, higher for more body).
  6. Turn the pump back on to recirculate wort through the grain bed. This improves efficiency and clarity. Adjust the flow so it's a gentle stream, not blasting -- you don't want to compact the grain bed.

Mash for 60 minutes. Some recipes call for different times, but 60 minutes is standard for most styles.

During the mash, the Brewzilla's temperature controller will maintain your set temp automatically. Keep an eye on it occasionally -- if it's overshooting or undershooting, you can adjust. Use your instant-read thermometer to verify temp in the grain bed itself vs. what the Brewzilla display says.

Optional: Mash out. After 60 minutes, some brewers raise the temperature to 168°F for 10 minutes to stop enzymatic activity and make the grain bed easier to drain. This is optional but can help with efficiency.

Step 3: Removing the malt pipe (lautering)

This is where the Brewzilla really shines compared to BIAB -- no heavy, dripping bag to wrestle with.

  1. Turn off the pump.
  2. Slowly lift the malt pipe out of the Brewzilla. It has handles for this. Let it drain back into the Brewzilla. The malt pipe sits on a bracket/lip so you can rest it above the wort and let it drain.
  3. Let it drain for 10-15 minutes. You can gently sparge (pour) some hot water (around 168°F) over the grain bed to rinse out extra sugars. Heat a separate pot of water for this, or keep some water hot in a kettle. Don't go crazy with sparge water -- you're mainly rinsing what's left behind.
  4. Remove the malt pipe entirely and set it aside. The spent grain goes in the compost or trash.

At this point you have your sweet wort in the Brewzilla ready for boiling.

Take a pre-boil gravity reading if you want to track your efficiency. Cool a small sample first so you get an accurate hydrometer reading.

Step 4: The boil

  1. Set the Brewzilla to boil. Crank both heating elements. It'll take a little while to reach a rolling boil on 120V -- be patient.
  2. Watch for the hot break. As the wort approaches boiling, a foamy head will form. This is protein coagulating. Watch it closely because it can boil over and make a huge mess. If it starts to rise, blow on it, stir it, or briefly kill the power. Once the hot break settles, you're in the clear.
  3. Start your 60 minute timer once you have a steady rolling boil.
  4. Add hops according to your schedule:
    • 60 min: Bittering hops go in at the start of the boil. These give your beer its bitterness (IBUs).
    • 15 min: Flavor hops. These contribute some bitterness but more hop flavor.
    • 5 min: Aroma hops. Mostly for aroma, minimal bitterness.
    • Flameout / 0 min: Whirlpool/flameout hops. Turn off the heat, add hops, and let them steep for 10-20 minutes. Big aroma and flavor, no bitterness.

Some recipes also call for additions like Irish moss or Whirlfloc at 15 minutes to help with clarity. Just follow your recipe.

Step 5: Cooling

As soon as the boil is done and you've finished any flameout hop additions:

  1. Drop your wort chiller (pre-sanitized -- I throw mine in for the last 15 minutes of the boil to sanitize it) into the Brewzilla.
  2. Hook up the cold water and start flowing. Stir the wort gently around the chiller to speed things up.
  3. Cool to below 80°F. This usually takes 20-30 minutes depending on your tap water temperature. In summer it takes longer.

The faster you cool, the better your cold break (protein precipitation) and the clearer your beer will be.

Step 6: Transfer to fermenter

  1. Sanitize everything that will touch the wort from here on -- fermenter, lid, airlock, strainer, anything.
  2. Use the Brewzilla's tap/valve to drain the wort through a sanitized strainer into your fermenter. The strainer catches hop debris and helps aerate the wort. You want aeration at this stage -- oxygen is good for yeast before fermentation starts.
  3. Leave the trub behind. The last bit of wort at the bottom of the Brewzilla is full of hop sludge and protein gunk (trub). Try to leave as much of this behind as you can. A little bit getting through is fine.
  4. Top up if needed. If you're short of 5 gallons in the fermenter, you can top up with pre-boiled or filtered water. Measure with the markings on your bucket.

Step 7: Pitch yeast

  1. Take an OG (Original Gravity) reading. Use your hydrometer and record this number -- you'll need it later to calculate ABV.
  2. Make sure the wort is below 80°F. Ideally around 65-70°F for ale yeast.
  3. Sprinkle your dry yeast on top of the wort. No need to rehydrate dry yeast -- just sprinkle it in. If using liquid yeast, follow the package instructions.
  4. Seal the fermenter with the lid, insert the bung, and put your airlock in (filled with water or sanitizer).
  5. Move the fermenter to a cool, dark spot where it can stay at a stable temperature (65-72°F for ales) for the next couple weeks.

Step 8: Clean up

Clean your Brewzilla and all your equipment while you've still got the energy. It's way harder to clean dried-on wort the next day.

  1. Rinse the Brewzilla thoroughly with hot water.
  2. Soak with OxiClean if there's stubborn buildup. Let it sit for 30 minutes, then scrub with a soft cloth (don't use abrasive pads on the stainless steel).
  3. Run clean water through the pump to flush it out. Use pipe cleaners on the fittings if needed.
  4. Clean the malt pipe -- knock out the spent grain, rinse, and scrub.
  5. Clean and dry everything else -- wort chiller, spoon, thermometer, etc.
  6. Bar Keepers Friend is great for making the stainless steel shine.
