Bottling

Bottling day is less exciting than brew day but just as important. Screw this up and you'll get flat beer, bottle bombs, or off-flavors. It takes about 1-2 hours. The key word for today is sanitize.

Before you start

Make sure:

  • Your gravity readings are stable (same reading 2-3 days apart) -- fermentation is done
  • You have enough clean bottles (about 48-50 twelve-ounce bottles for a 5 gallon batch)
  • You have bottle caps
  • You have a bottle capper
  • You have priming sugar (corn sugar / dextrose)
  • You have your bottling wand and tubing

Step 1: Sanitize everything

Everything that touches the beer from here on must be sanitized with Star San.

  • Bottles (soak or use a bottle rinser/tree)
  • Bottle caps
  • Bottling wand and tubing
  • Spigot on your fermenter
  • Anything else that might contact the beer

A bottle rinser/sanitizer pump (the kind you mount to a bottle tree) makes this way faster. You can also batch-sanitize bottles in a bucket of Star San solution. Let them soak for a couple minutes, drain upside down on a rack or bottle tree, and you're good. Don't rinse Star San off -- "don't fear the foam."

Step 2: Make your priming solution

Priming sugar is what carbonates your beer in the bottle. The yeast eats the sugar, produces CO2, and since the bottle is sealed, the CO2 dissolves into the beer.

  1. Calculate the amount of priming sugar. This depends on the style and volume of beer. A good starting point for most ales is 3/4 cup (about 4-5 oz by weight) of corn sugar (dextrose) for a 5 gallon batch. Use an online priming calculator for precision -- you'll need to know your beer volume and target carbonation level.
  2. Boil the sugar in about 2 cups of water for 5 minutes. This sanitizes it and dissolves it evenly.
  3. Let it cool for a few minutes.

Do NOT over-prime. Too much sugar = too much CO2 = bottles exploding. This is not a joke. Use a priming calculator and measure carefully. When in doubt, use less.

Step 3: Add priming sugar to beer

You have two options:

Option A: Bottling bucket method (recommended)

  1. Pour the cooled priming solution into a clean, sanitized bucket.
  2. Transfer the beer from your fermenter into this bottling bucket on top of the priming solution. Use the spigot on your fermenter and let it flow gently down the side of the bottling bucket to minimize splashing (splashing = oxygen = bad at this stage).
  3. Gently stir with a sanitized spoon to distribute the sugar evenly. Don't go crazy -- you want to mix it without aerating the beer.

Option B: Direct to fermenter

If you only have one bucket, you can carefully pour the priming solution directly into the fermenter and gently stir. This works but risks uneven distribution and disturbing the sediment at the bottom.

Step 4: Fill the bottles

  1. Attach the bottling wand to the spigot via a short piece of tubing.
  2. Open the spigot.
  3. Insert the wand into a bottle and press the tip against the bottom -- beer flows.
  4. Fill until the beer reaches the very top of the bottle. When you pull the wand out, the displacement will leave about the right amount of headspace (~1 inch).
  5. Repeat 48 more times. It gets tedious. Put on some music.

Set up an assembly line -- it goes way faster. Sanitized bottles lined up, fill them in a row, then cap them in a row. Have some paper towels down because drips are inevitable.

Step 5: Cap the bottles

  1. Place a sanitized cap on each filled bottle.
  2. Use your capper to crimp the cap on. Give each one a firm press -- you want a tight seal. Give the cap a little twist after capping to make sure it's snug and not spinning freely.
  3. Check your work. If a cap looks crooked or loose, re-cap it.

Step 6: Conditioning

This is the waiting game.

  1. Store the bottles upright at room temperature (68-75°F) in a dark place. A closet or box works great.
  2. Wait at least 2 weeks. The yeast needs time to eat the priming sugar and carbonate the beer. Two weeks is the minimum for most ales.
  3. 3 weeks is better. Some styles benefit from even longer conditioning.

After 2 weeks, throw a couple bottles in the fridge to chill. Pop one open and see how the carbonation is. If it's still flat, give it another week at room temperature.

The beer will improve with time. Even after carbonation is done, the flavors continue to develop. A beer that tastes rough at 2 weeks might be great at 4 weeks. Be patient.

Troubleshooting

Flat beer (no carbonation):

  • Bottles haven't been warm enough -- move them somewhere warmer
  • Not enough time -- give it another week or two
  • Not enough priming sugar -- not much you can do except wait longer
  • Dead yeast -- rare with normal-strength beers, but possible with high ABV brews. You can add a tiny amount of fresh yeast at bottling time for big beers

Over-carbonated / gushers:

  • Too much priming sugar
  • Bottled before fermentation was truly done (always confirm with stable gravity readings!)
  • Possible infection adding extra fermentable sugars
  • If bottles are over-carbed, refrigerate them all immediately -- cold slows yeast activity. Open carefully over a sink.

Off-flavors:

  • Oxidation (cardboard taste) -- too much splashing during transfer or too much headspace
  • Sourness or funk -- likely an infection from poor sanitation
  • Astringent / cidery -- possibly too much priming sugar, especially if you used table sugar instead of corn sugar

Clean up

  1. Clean your fermenter, bottling bucket, tubing, and wand with OxiClean. Don't let the gunk dry on.
  2. Rinse everything thoroughly and let it air dry.
  3. Store your equipment clean and dry for next time.

Now crack one of the beers from your last batch while you wait for this one. Cheers.
