# Fermenting

You pitched your yeast, sealed up your fermenter, and put it somewhere cool and dark. Now the yeast does its thing. This section covers what to expect and what to do (and not do) during fermentation.

---

## The first 24-48 hours

Within 12-24 hours of pitching yeast, you should start seeing signs of fermentation:

- **Airlock activity** -- bubbles coming through the airlock every few seconds. This is CO2 escaping.
- **Kraeusen** -- a thick, foamy layer on top of the beer. This is normal and healthy. It can look pretty gnarly but that's fine.

If you don't see any activity after 24 hours, don't panic yet. Give it another 12 hours. If nothing after 48 hours, you might have a problem (dead yeast, too cold, etc.) and may need to pitch more yeast.

!!!
**Do NOT open the fermenter** to check on it unless absolutely necessary. Every time you open it you risk introducing oxygen and bacteria. Trust the airlock.
!!!

---

## Primary fermentation

This is the main event. The yeast is converting sugars into alcohol and CO2.

- **Timeline:** Most ales ferment out in **5-7 days**. Some are done in 3-4 days, especially with healthy yeast and good temperature control.
- **Temperature:** Keep it as stable as possible. For most ale yeasts (US-05, S-04), aim for **65-72°F (18-22°C)**. Temperature swings cause off-flavors. A basement or closet with stable temps is ideal.
- **Leave it alone.** Seriously. Don't shake it, don't move it, don't open it. Let the yeast work.

After about 5-7 days, the vigorous bubbling will slow down and the kraeusen will start to fall back into the beer. This doesn't necessarily mean fermentation is complete -- use your hydrometer to confirm.

---

## Taking gravity readings

This is how you know when fermentation is actually done.

1. **Sanitize** your hydrometer, flask/test jar, and anything else that touches the beer.
2. **Pull a sample** from the spigot on your fermenter. Fill the flask enough to float the hydrometer.
3. **Check the temperature** of the sample -- hydrometers are calibrated for a specific temperature (usually 60°F). Use an online calculator to adjust the reading if your sample is warmer.
4. **Read the gravity** at the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of the liquid).
5. **Record it.**
6. **Wait 2-3 days** and take another reading.
7. **If the readings are the same** (or within 0.001), fermentation is complete. Your beer has reached **Final Gravity (FG)**.

!!!
Your recipe should tell you the expected FG. Most standard ales finish around **1.008-1.014**. If your FG is way higher than expected, fermentation may have stalled. Give it more time, warm it up a few degrees, or gently swirl the fermenter to rouse the yeast.
!!!

**Calculating ABV:** Use your OG and FG with this formula:
**ABV = (OG - FG) x 131.25**

Example: OG 1.055, FG 1.012 = (0.055 - 0.012) x 131.25 = **5.64% ABV**

Or just use an online calculator. Brewersfriend.com has a good one.

## Dry hopping

If your recipe calls for dry hops (common in IPAs, pale ales, NEIPAs), here's how:

### The magnet method

1. **Sanitize** your dry hop bag and food-grade magnets.
2. **Put your measured hops** in the mesh bag. Put a magnet inside the bag with the hops.
3. **Open the fermenter** (quickly and carefully -- minimize exposure time).
4. **Hang the bag** from the underside of the lid using the magnet on the outside of the lid to hold the one inside the bag. Close the lid immediately.
5. **Wait 2-3 days.** When ready, simply remove the external magnet. The bag drops into the beer and the hops start doing their thing.
6. **Leave the hops in for 3-5 days** then proceed to bottling. You don't want to dry hop for too long -- it can extract grassy, vegetal flavors.

### The simple method

If you don't have magnets, you can just open the fermenter and toss the hop bag directly into the beer. Close it back up immediately. Some people just throw loose hops in without a bag (it works but makes a mess during bottling).

### DDH (Double Dry Hop)

Some recipes call for two rounds of dry hops added at different times. Same process, just repeated. Usually the first charge goes in during active fermentation, and the second goes in after fermentation is done.

## How long to ferment

A general timeline for a typical ale:

Day | What's happening
--- | ---
1-3 | Active fermentation, vigorous bubbling, kraeusen forming
4-7 | Fermentation slowing, kraeusen falling
7-10 | Take gravity readings to confirm FG
7-14 | Dry hop if applicable (3-5 days before bottling)
10-14 | Ready to bottle (once FG is stable)

**Don't rush it.** Leaving beer on the yeast for a few extra days won't hurt anything. The yeast will clean up after themselves (reabsorbing off-flavor compounds like diacetyl). Going too long (months) in a bucket can lead to oxidation, but 2-3 weeks is totally fine.

---

## Troubleshooting

**No airlock activity:** Check for leaks in the lid seal. Bucket lids are notorious for not sealing well. The beer might be fermenting fine but CO2 is escaping around the lid instead of through the airlock. Check gravity to confirm.

**Fermentation seems stuck:** If gravity is higher than expected FG and hasn't moved in several days:
- Warm it up a few degrees
- Gently swirl the fermenter to rouse settled yeast
- If that doesn't work, pitch more yeast

**Smells like rotten eggs:** Some yeast strains (especially lager yeast) produce sulfur during fermentation. It usually clears up on its own. Leave it alone.

**Mold on top:** If you see fuzzy, colored mold (not just yeast kraeusen which can look weird), your beer is likely infected. This is rare if you sanitized properly. You can try to salvage it but honestly, dump it and figure out where your sanitation failed.

When your gravity readings are stable and any dry hopping is done, head over to [Bottling](/process/bottling).
